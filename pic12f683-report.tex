\begin{abstract}
This report describes an extensive analysis and reverse engineering of the Microchip PIC12F683 microcontroller.

All analysis and sample preparation was performed by the author using garage laboratory facilities and open source software for professional development, entertainment, and educational purposes only; this project was not supported or sponsored by any third party.

Sample preparation was limited to basic wet chemistry and optical microscopy, to make the project more fun and challenging and to push the limits of what could be done in a home setup. SEM/FIB, dry etch, precision polishing, industry reverse engineering software, and other advanced tools were deliberately not used, even if the author had access to them via work or friends.

This report was produced as a companion to \href{https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yw8KQuswcAE&list=PLBNA3N3RCc8oRO_tnl_lnhJU61hRJ6qbU&index=1}{a YouTube series} by the author, which includes video of sample preparation activities and narrated analysis as well as various background material helpful to novices interested in semiconductor reverse engineering. The report provides additional technical detail and high resolution imagery which did not lend itself well to the video format.

This report is licensed under CC BY-ND 4.0 and the LaTeX source and images used in the report can be found
\href{https://github.com/azonenberg/pic12f683-analysis}{on GitHub}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Motivation}

Why go through all this trouble? I'm not being paid to write this, none of my customers at work use this part, I haven't done a new design with it in years, and my YouTube channel isn't even monetized.

In short, this is a combination of a nerdsnipe\footnote{\href{https://xkcd.com/356/}{XKCD 356}} and a homage to my roots - the first MCU I ever used as a brand-new student embedded developer in 2008, and the first MCU I ever broke the code protection on as a brand-new hardware reverse engineer in 2011. I've wanted to go back and study it in extreme detail ever since.

Plus it's a convenient vintage part for this sort of project: old and large enough for full circuit analysis to be possible without SEM, but still a vaguely modern planarized standard cell architecture rather than ancient stuff made on Rubylith where every transistor looks different from the one next to it and metal layers bend and sag as they cross.

\section{Device Overview}

The subject of this report is the Microchip PIC12F683-I/MD. Multiple specimens, all from the same cut tape purchased at Digi-Key with date code 2321 and lot code 9BW, were used during the analysis.

The PIC12F683 is an 8-bit microcontroller from Microchip Technology, originally developed in 2004\footnote{Based on the revision date of the oldest available documentation on Microchip's website} and still in production. It is a strict Harvard architecture with no access to flash from the data bus and no ability to execute code from RAM. The CPU is a microcoded sequential design with a maximum frequency of 20 MHz, which executes at 4 clocks per instruction for all operations other than branches which take longer. Function calls use a hardware stack with a maximum depth of eight nested calls.

It has 2048 14-bit instruction words of flash, 128 bytes of SRAM, and 256 bytes of EEPROM. The EEPROM is indirectly accessible via address/data/control registers; EEPROM data is not directly memory mapped.

It is available in four packages, all with eight pins (one power, one ground, and six I/Os): DIP (ordering code P), 3.9mm SOIC (SN), 4x4mm DFN (MD), and 6x5mm DFN (MF). Pin numbering between the packages is identical.

Due to the extremely low pin count, in-circuit debug of the PIC12F683 is typically impractical (three of the six pins must be used for program/debug). A special in-circuit emulation device exists (only available on a special test fixture and not sold standalone) which breaks the debug signals out on separate bond pads allowing non-intrusive debug with full access to all six IOs. This emulation die is a separate mask set and will not be discussed further in this report.

\section{Package Analysis}

The PIC12F683 is available in multiple packages; the subject of this report used the ``MD" package which is an 8-pin saw-singulated DFN with nominal dimensions of 4 x 4 x 0.9 mm.

\subsection{PCN Search}

A search of vendor product change notifications (PCNs) was conducted, which suggested that numerous assembly sites (designated MP3A, MTAI, NSEB, GTK, LPI, UNIS, and MMT) were used to produce this part and it was not possible to identify with certainty which one was used for the samples under test without access to internal Microchip lot traceability information or supplier databases.

A new PFAS-free die attach material ``QMI519" was qualified in September 2025\footnote{\href{https://www.microchip.com/PCNWebAPI/api/pcn/DownloadPcnDocument?pcnid=24234&fileName=PFAS\%20Elimination\%20and\%20Die\%20Attach_Explanation.pdf}{PCN CENO-16EGCZ399}} however the samples used for this analysis had 2023 week 21 date codes and thus presumably were using the older, PFAS-containing, die attach. One PCNs mentioned CRM1076DJ die attach and G770HCD mold compound\footnote{\href{https://www.microchip.com/PCNWebAPI/api/pcn/DownloadPcnDocument?pcnId=252&affectedcpns=pdf}{PCN CYER-19MGTD414}} for the 4x4 DFN; another mentioned 8600 die attach and G700LTD mold compound\footnote{\href{https://www.microchip.com/PCNWebAPI/api/pcn/DownloadPcnDocument?pcnid=6667&fileName=PCN_JAON-04FCXY806_Qual\%20Report.pdf}{PCN JAON-04FCXY806}} so any of these are likely candidates. The author's lab capabilities were not sufficient to determine with certainty which was used. Based on these and other PCNs the bond wire material was likely to be palladium coated copper and may or may not have a gold flash over the palladium plating, depending on which assembly site was used\footnote{In the author's experience, older generation PIC12F683s used gold ball bonding. The samples analyzed in this report were copper bonded and PCNs reference both CuPd and CuPdAu bond wire. Without access to SEM+EDS or similar high resolution spectroscopy capabilities, it was not possible to detect the presence or absence of the gold flash layer.}.

\subsection{Package Overview}

One sample was photographed at low magnification using a combination of through-objective and low-angle illumination to best highlight the various materials in the package with vastly different albedos.

The top side of the package (Fig. \ref{pkgtop}) was laser marked ``12F683 I/MD (e3) 2321 9BW" plus a pin 1 marker and had no other noteworthy features.

The bottom side of the package (Fig. \ref{pkgbot}) had eight tin-plated copper solder lands plus a die attach/thermal paddle with a mitered corner to denote the pin 1 position. A small amount of deformation was noted at the outboard edges of the lands, consistent with smearing of the relatively soft copper alloy by the dicing saw during the singulation process. Witness marks (Fig. \ref{probe-witness}, \ref{probe-witness2}), consistent with impressions of probe/socket contacts during factory test, could be seen on each terminal. The witness marks consisted of two circular dots approximately 37 \unit{\micro\meter} in diameter and varied slightly in spacing, suggesting that they were the result of multiple contact events with a single contact per pin (or a socket with two individual contacts per pin) rather than a single forked contact.

\subsection{Cross Section}

One sample was placed in a standard 1.5 inch metallographic mold (Fig. \ref{mount}) and embedded in Ted Pella AcryliMet
resin for cross sectioning, then rough ground with P180 SiC paper until a few hundred \unit{\micro\meter} from the die.
Increasingly finer grades (P400, P800, P1200) were used for the final approach to the die and P2500 was used for the
final grinding stage to bring the cross section plane into the desired location (bond pads along the east edge of the
device). Final polishing used 1 \unit{\micro\meter} diamond and 50 \unit{\nano\meter} colloidal alumina.

\begin{figure}[h]
\includegraphics[width=10cm,keepaspectratio]{images/mount2.jpg}
\caption{Embedded package sample}
\label{mount}
\end{figure}

The polisher being used for the analysis did not have precision tip/tilt adjustment, so it was not possible to get the entire row of bond pads in view simultaneously. The sectioning plane (Fig. \ref{section-plane}) was approximately 1.2\degree off perpendicular to the die; providing excellent views of the pin 5 and 6 wire bonds (Fig. \ref{section}) as well as the metal stackup on the pin 7 and 8 bond pads, however the wire bonds on pins 7 and 8 were not captured by the section.

The section plane was chosen to get as many bond pads as possible in view, however this did mean that the second bonds and solder lands on the leadframe were not visible. This was not judged sufficiently important to justify the effort of preparing a second section with a 90\degree perpendicular plane to the one shown here.

The die was connected to the leadframe with copper ball bonds (Fig. \ref{bond}). Bond pads consisted of three layers of aluminum metallization with vias connecting all three layers at the perimeter. A small amount of metal squeeze-out was seen around the bonds, but did not appear to be excessive and there was no sign of damage or deformation to the lower metal layers in the pad structure.

The die was approximately 200 \unit{\micro\meter} thick and mounted to a 200 \unit{\micro\meter} thick copper thermal paddle by a metal-filled organic adhesive, likely silver epoxy (Fig. \ref{die-attach}). The bond line was approximately 6.7 \unit{\micro\meter} thick and the fillet extended another 8.5 \unit{\micro\meter} above the bottom edge of the die at the left corner.

The bottom surface of the thermal paddle was plated in approximately 13.6 \unit{\micro\meter} of tin (Fig. \ref{paddle-corner}). The plating significantly overhung the paddle at the edges and conformed to the surface of the molding compound in the overhang region, suggesting that the leadframe had been bare, or perhaps plated in a thin flash layer of some sort, prior to package assembly and then a thick tin layer was applied after overmolding. A very thin Cu-Sn intermetallic layer was barely visible at the interface but the resolution of the optical microscope (roughly 300 \unit{\nano\meter}) was insufficient to make any remotely accurate measurements of its thickness.

\section{Process Analysis}

The die is made on Microchip's in-house 160K wafer
technology\footnote{\href{https://www.microchip.com/PCNWebAPI/api/pcn/DownloadPcnDocument?pcnId=10087&affectedcpns=pdf}{PCN
KSRA-19MSVX727}} at Fab 4 in Gresham, OR.

\subsection{Stackup}

This is a planarized 350nm class process with three aluminum metal layers and two poly layers (Fig. \ref{xsec-seal}), supporting floating-gate embedded Flash memory. Vias and contacts are tungsten. Inspection of the seal ring in cross section clearly shows all three metal layers connected by vias to each other and the substrate, as well as poly. No connections to poly in the seal ring were visible in the cross section.

All metal layers are approximately equal in thickness and spacing: approximately 1000 \unit{\nano\meter} thick and 800 \unit{\nano\meter} apart, with M1 approximately 1300 \unit{\nano\meter} above the substrate. Poly is approximately 425 \unit{\nano\meter} thick\footnote{Given the 300 \unit{\nano\meter} optical resolution of the microscope, there are large error bounds on all of these measurements.}. The top barrier/adhesion layer was not clearly seen during the analysis. Bottom barrier was readily exposed during deprocessing and often was easier to image than the metal itself. No spectroscopic analysis of the bottom barrier was performed due to lack of the required laboratory capabilities, but the color was consistent with titanium nitride (TiN).

\subsection{Passivation}

The die is covered in a thick orange-colored overglass (Fig. \ref{logic}) which coats the top and sidewalls of metal 3, growing in thickness until the gaps between adjacent conductors are completely filled and only a thin line can be seen between them. This reduces contrast between wires and makes them more difficult to follow, so removal of the passivation is beneficial even for tracing out signals on M3 (compare Fig. \ref{logic} and \ref{vias}).

\subsection{Design rules}

All three metal layers have similar or identical design rules, as is typical for processes of this era (there are not enough layers to dedicate some thin ones to high-density local interconnect and have thicker ones for long-range power and signal routing), with an observed minimum pitch of approximately 1.75 \unit{\micro\meter} and roughly equal conductor size and gap size.

Unlike most more modern processes (in which conductors are relatively constant width even at vias), both the top and bottom conductors have a significantly enlarged annular ring / landing pad at vias (Fig. \ref{vias}). This enables the locations of vias to be inferred from only a metal layer image, without the need to deprocess specifically to a via layer.

Dummy fill for CMP is present on all metal layers. The dummy fill pattern consists of parallel lines the same size as signal conductors (Fig. \ref{dummyfill}), laid out on the same grid as signal conductors, which makes it difficult to distinguish from actual signal routing at first glance. Dummy fill lines are never placed directly adjacent to signal lines (perhaps for crosstalk/coupling reasons); a single routing channel of clearance is always left between dummy fill and active conductors. This, plus the fact that dummy metal is always floating (no vias) is sufficient to allow it to be reliably identified.

Excessively long dummy fill lines (roughly 80 \unit{\micro\meter} and up) have short gaps inserted, as seen in the top center of Fig. \ref{dummyfill}.

\section{Decapsulation and Deprocessing}

Five devices were fully decapsulated with 70\% nitric acid to remove the package and bond wires.

Two samples were deprocessed with a commercially available aqueous solution of unbuffered 1-3\% hydrofluoric acid\footnote{Whink rust stain remover. Concentration data is from the SDS; the author has not performed a titration to determine the actual concentration more precisely.} due to lack of availability of buffered oxide etch\footnote{The author has since acquired a 40\% ammonium fluoride solution and will be conducting tests with buffered etchants to see if improved deprocessing results are obtained with it.}. The first sample was used for preliminary etch rate characterization, while the second was used for all imagery in this report. The remaining three dice were held as reserves in the as-decapsulated state in case additional data was needed due to overetch, scratches, etc but have not been used as of this writing.

A total of 27 etch passes (numbered 1 to 27) were performed, photographing on a Labsmore LIP-X1 CNC microscope platform after each pass. After 14 of the etch passes, mechanical cleaning operations (e.g. sonication) were performed to remove free-floating debris and a second imaging cycle (denoted with a ``B" suffix) was performed, for a total of 41 documented deprocessing steps.

All 41 deprocessing steps were imaged at moderate resolution with a Mitutoyo 20x/0.42 objective. Each 20x scan consisted of approximately 42 (6 x 7 X/Y positions at a single focus level)\footnote{The exact number of tiles varied slightly due to variations in alignment of the sample on the microscope stage, which resulted in a $\pm 1$ tile jitter in array size in each axis from one scan to the next.} image tiles. Additionally, 22 of the deprocessing steps were also imaged at high resolution with a Mitutoyo 100x/0.90 objective. Each 100x scan consisted of approximately 13260 (30 x 34 X/Y positions at 13 focus levels) image tiles. The complete dataset including the cross section sample and all deprocessing stages was 203.5 GB and 369971 photos and required over 125 hours of microscope time to acquire.

Several of the 20x overview scans are shown in this section as representative examples. These are reduced from the full resolution to avoid an overly large PDF; full resolution of all 20x scans as well as focus-stacked versions\footnote{Raw tiles have not been uploaded due to lack of a good sharing setup for multi-level imagery. The current focus stacks lose some contrast vs the raw tiles; all circuit tracings in this report used raw tiles hand-selected for the best contrast of the specific features of interest.} of the 100x scans is available \href{https://siliconprawn.org/map/microchip/pic12f683/}{on siliconprawn}. All 41 deprocessing steps, not just the ones shown in this section, were considered when choosing imagery for circuit extractions; due to the inherent nonuniformities of wet etch deprocessing sometimes a specific feature was most visible a few etch cycles before or after another feature on the same metal layer.
